# On AI plagiarism - No one can own a style.  

AI, ethics, law  

---

In this thought, I will be discussing a very sensitive topic. AI and intellectual property.  
As AI systems have become a significant part of the modern man's life, 
it has come to the general population's attention that generative models are 
trained on data and intellectual property without the consent of the property owner.  
This has sparked a global outrage, especially among those who dedicate their life to 
generating such content (i.e. data), and has steered a popular narative that this is infringement
of the holders right, which will have a significant impact in the democratic world where
the popular opinion dictates what will be enforced. 
I view this as problematic becuase it lacks a rational motivation of copyright, or intellectual property infringement
and could negatively impact our future, freedom, technological advancement and even national security.  


Observing people with little to no knowledge on generative AI speak out on their rights being infringed as a result of training reminds me of a children's book by Alf Prøysen: "A Little Goat That Could Count Till Ten". The book is about a kid goat who runs around and counts the other animals. The other animals do not know what counting is, and in confused frustration, they get angry at the little goat for counting them without their consent. And so, they start chasing him. The kid eventually hops on a boat, and as the other animals follow, the parrot captain voices his concern that the ship will sink if there are more than 10 animals onboard. Suddenly they need someone who can count, and the kid comes to the rescue counting all the animals, which turned out to be a total of exactly 10. The story ends with the other animals forgiving and celebrating the kid for his counting ability as it turned out to not only be harmless, but also useful.  

Ok, how is this analogous at all? Much "intellectual property" is freely available for anyone to view on the internet, as decided by the creators themselves. Anyone can view this content, take inspiration and create derivative work. But if there is some program that views this and creates derivative works, it suddenly feels like theft. Is it? It is if the same work created by a human would be considered theft. 
You see, plagiarism is about content similiarty. The threshold is not something that has been formally assigned, but if you copy 90% of the words in a novel verbatim it would, of course, obviously be plagiarism. So yes, training on any IP __CAN__ be plagiarism, but is not necessarily.

So similar to how one needs to understand counting, we need to understand: what are we trying to do when we are training a model? When training a model, we specifically **don't** want the model to memorize the material. We want it to learn general patterns that make up language, art, code, or whatever type of information the data represents. We want the model to generalize. Overfitting, is specifically the concept of memorizing the exact data, which is what we are trying to avoid. If we avoid overfitting, the model will not generate plagiarism. The model will have taken inspiration of the training data, but it will not have copied in a way . 




An adjacent topic is generative art being created in the style of an artist. I have observed multiple instances where, if the style has sufficient resemblance of some creator associated with it, 
it strengthens the sentiment of plagiarism by AI. But can anyone own a style? This would be an absurd and unjustifiable complication to the concept of intellectual property. Just as ridicolous as people or companies attempting to claim ownership to a color or a word. Take [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DVmoIiqcmZc), where the youtuber __Imaginary ambition__ creates a song in the style of Death Grips. And he is within his full right to do so.   


As the creator of an image, you own this. But you don't own this.
